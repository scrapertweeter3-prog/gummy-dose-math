A 10 milligram gummy is twenty doses in one bite

The supplement shelf prices milligrams as if they all go to the same place, and nothing on it hides the spread like melatonin. A jar of 60 sleep gummies at 10 milligrams each runs $13.20, which is 22 cents a night and 2.2 cents per milligram. A bottle of 120 half-milligram tablets runs $9.00, 7.5 cents a night, 15 cents per milligram. The gummy wins the per-milligram fight by 6.8 times, and the shelf reads that as the better deal. But a dose is a quantity, not a price, and the quantity the sleep research works with is the small one. One gummy is twenty tablets in a single bite.

The ledger runs 3,650 nights, one decade. The gummy habit costs $803.00. The tablet habit costs $273.75. The gummy wins by $529.25 in dollars and ships 36,500 milligrams against the tablet bottle's 1,825, which is 34,675 milligrams of extra hormone nobody asked for, 9.5 more every night. Priced at the tablet bottle's 15 cents per milligram, the surplus would list at $5,201.25. The gummy is not selling hormone so much as bundling it: the marginal milligram is nearly free, which is exactly why labels drift upward to 10, 15, 20 while the studied quantity stays put.

None of this says the gummy fails, or that the compound does. It says the pricing rewards the overshoot, so the cheap-per-milligram option quietly becomes the high-dose option. The one knob that matters is choosing 0.5 on purpose, because the arithmetic of the bottle should decide your dose only if you let it.
